Field note

Underpricing is fawn

The founder who "can't" raise their prices isn't bad at maths. A price is a boundary, and boundaries are a body event.

Let me tell you about a price conversation. Composite, but I've had some version of it maybe forty times.

A coach — brilliant, booked out, genuinely changes people's lives — sits across from me. She charges €80 an hour. She has a waitlist. The maths on this is not subtle. Every consultant she knows charges triple. Her clients would pay triple and thank her for the privilege. She knows all of this. She has known it for two years.

So I ask the simple question. "What would happen if you charged €240?"

And I watch her body answer before her mouth does. Something crosses her face. Her throat does a little swallow. Her hand comes up to her collarbone, not on purpose, just lands there. And she says, in a smaller voice than she's used the whole session, "I couldn't. It feels... greedy. Who am I to charge that?"

There it is. That's not a pricing problem. That's a fawn response, live, in the room.

The fourth F

Let me back up, because "fawn" is the one most people haven't heard of. We all know fight, flight, and freeze. Fawn is the fourth F, and it's the sneakiest, because it looks like being nice. When a threat is too big to fight or flee and freezing won't help, some nervous systems learned a fourth trick: appease it. Please it. Make yourself so agreeable, so useful, so undemanding that the dangerous thing decides you're not worth hurting. Keep everyone happy and you stay safe. Keep everyone happy and you belong.

It's a survival strategy, and for a lot of people it was a very good one, once. If you grew up somewhere that a raised voice meant danger, or love came with conditions, or being "too much" got you rejected, then reading the room and shrinking yourself to fit it wasn't a personality flaw. It was competence. It kept you safe. Ten-year-old you deserves a medal.

The problem is that thirty-eight-year-old founder-you is still running it, and now it's costing you a fortune.

A price is a boundary

Because a price is a boundary. It's you saying: this is what my work is worth, this is what it costs to have me, and if that's too much, that's okay, we're not a fit. That last part is the killer. A price is a place where you actively risk someone being disappointed in you. Someone frowning. Someone saying "that's expensive" and going elsewhere. For a nervous system organised around never letting anyone be unhappy with you, that risk doesn't feel like business. It feels like danger. Same ancient inbox, remember. Leopard, hostile email, unhappy prospect — the body barely tells them apart.

So the underpricing coach isn't bad at maths. Her maths is perfect. Her body has simply vetoed the number, because the number requires her to tolerate the possibility of someone's disappointment, and her whole system was built to prevent exactly that at any cost. She's not choosing €80. Her nervous system is choosing safety, and €80 is what safety happens to cost this year. About €160 an hour of it, times a full calendar. That adds up to a house deposit she's donating to her own fear.

You can spot fawn all over a business once you know the shape of it. The scope creep you never charge for. The "sure, I can squeeze that in" that you resent by Thursday. The discount you offer before they even ask, so nobody has to feel the friction of the real number. The invoice you send three weeks late because sending it feels pushy. The client you should have fired a quarter ago but can't, because letting them go means letting them be upset with you. All of it is the same move: keep the other person comfortable, absorb the cost into your own body, call it being professional.

The fear is wearing the mask of virtue, which is a hell of a disguise.

And here's what makes fawn so hard to catch: it feels good. It feels generous. It feels like being a nice person with strong values about not being greedy. You get to avoid the terror of the boundary and feel morally superior while you do it. No wonder it's stubborn.

Making it survivable

So when someone tells me "I've tried to raise my prices, I just can't seem to do it," I don't reach for a pricing framework. They don't need a framework. They could build me a pricing framework. We go to the body instead. What actually happens, physically, in the half-second you imagine saying the bigger number out loud? Where does it live? The throat, usually. The chest. That hand that flies up to the collarbone like it's protecting something.

Then we make it survivable. Not "just charge more, you deserve it" — that's a slogan, and slogans bounce off a braced body like rain off a tent. We shrink it until the body can bear it. Maybe you don't overhaul your whole pricing. Maybe you just say the new number out loud, to me, once, and we sit with what happens. Maybe you quote it to exactly one new lead, someone you don't even care about landing, purely as an experiment in tolerating the moment. The goal of that first rep isn't the money. It's the discovery — burned into the body, not just understood in the head — that you said the bigger number and the other person was maybe slightly taken aback and you did not die. Nobody left. The sky held.

That's the whole thing. A boundary is a body's discovery that it can survive someone else's disappointment. You can't think your way there. You have to feel it, once, with support, and then again, until your system updates the file from "danger" to "Tuesday."

The coach I mentioned? She didn't triple her price that day. She said "two hundred and forty euros" out loud to me, her voice wobbled, her hand did the collarbone thing, and then she laughed — this surprised, slightly giddy laugh — because some part of her felt the ground stay solid. That laugh was the update starting. The new price came a few weeks later. The body went first.
