Field note

Always busy, never clear

The founder who does forty things a day and couldn't tell you what any of them were for

I have a friend. I won't use his real name, partly to protect him and partly because he's actually about five people I know wearing one coat. Call him David. (Composite, obviously. But you'll know him.)

David runs a business that makes real money. Eighteen grand in a good month. By every visible measure he is killing it. And David is busy. My god, is he busy. He wakes up already behind. By nine in the morning he's cleared eleven Slack messages, opened three proposals, half-started a new landing page, said yes to a podcast, and reorganised his to-do app for the second time this week because the old setup "wasn't quite working."

Ask David what he did today and he'll say "so much." Ask him what actually moved the business and he goes quiet.

That quiet is the whole post.

Here's what I want you to notice, and I say it with love, because for a long stretch of my own life I was David: busyness feels like progress. It has all the sensations of progress. The emptied inbox, the closed tabs, that little hit when you tick something off. Your body reads motion as safety. If I'm moving, nothing can catch me.

But motion isn't direction. A washing machine is extremely busy and it never goes anywhere.

A David day, from the inside

Let me walk you through it, because I think you might recognise the furniture.

He sits down at 8:40 with a coffee and one clear intention: today I finish the proposal for the client who could double the quarter. He opens the document. He reads the first line he wrote three weeks ago. Something in his chest does a small tightening thing, nothing dramatic, just a flicker. And then — watch how fast this happens — a Slack pings, and he's grateful. Genuinely grateful. He answers it. Then a second one. Then he "just quickly" checks whether that ad is still running. Then he has a great idea for a lead magnet and opens a fresh doc to capture it because ideas are precious. By the time he looks up it's 12:30, he's done fourteen things, and the proposal is exactly where it was at 8:40. Untouched. Judging him quietly from its tab.

Six projects on the go. Forty tasks flagged. Permanent motion. Zero landing.

If that felt a bit too specific, good. Sit with the discomfort for a second. That's not a laziness problem, and it's definitely not a productivity-app problem, though David will try to solve it with a new one roughly every fortnight.

It's flight.

Flight in a business costume

Flight is one of the oldest programs the body runs. An animal senses a threat it can't fight, so it moves — fast, and away. In the savannah the threat is a leopard. In your business the "threat" is a proposal, a price, a hard conversation, one unglamorous task with your name on it that the body has quietly filed under danger. And the beautiful, maddening thing about flight in a modern business is that it doesn't look like fear. It looks like hustle. It looks productive. You get to run from the one scary thing by sprinting toward forty safe ones, and the world claps for how hard you're working.

The tell is the exhaustion. Real progress tires you and then satisfies you. Flight just tires you.

David goes to bed wrung out and wakes up with the same undone thing sitting on his chest, and he cannot for the life of him understand why, because look how much he did.

I lived in a version of this for years. Mine had a soundtrack of open browser tabs. At one point I had a smartwatch buzzing me every two hours just to check whether I was actually in my body or three miles ahead of it, because I genuinely could not tell anymore. The engineer in me built a machine to detect the problem the mammal in me was creating. Which is funny, and also was not a joke at the time.

A three-line experiment

So here's a small experiment. It takes three lines and about ninety seconds, at the end of a day. No app required. Please, no app.

Line one: what did I actually do today — the real list, all of it, the reorganising and the "quick checks" included.

Line two: which single thing on that list, if it had been the only thing I did, would still have made today a genuinely good day for the business?

Line three: did I do that one thing? Yes or no. And if no — what did I do instead of it, and what did my body do in the two seconds before I bolted?

Do it for five days. Don't fix anything. Just collect the data, like a scientist who's slightly afraid of the results. Most people find the same starred thing gets skipped four days out of five, and that the skip always comes with a tiny physical flinch they'd never noticed before. A shallow breath. A shoulders-creeping-toward-ears thing. The body voting not that one half a second before the mind invents a reason.

That flinch is not in the way of the work. That flinch is the work. It's the most honest signal you've got about where the real ceiling is. Not your strategy — you know your strategy. The ceiling is made of you.

David doesn't need a better system. David needs to find out what his body thinks is a leopard, and why, and how to settle it enough that the proposal stops reading as a predator. That's a different kind of work than "try harder tomorrow." Trying harder just makes a fast runner faster.

You're allowed to be busy. Busy is fine. Busy and clear is a great life. Busy and never clear is a nervous system running from something, dressed up as a founder crushing it.
