Field note

Business gets rewarded for fight and flight

The world claps for the exact state that's slowly wrecking you. Success first, bill later.

Let me start with an animal fact, because it explains more about your calendar than most business books.

An animal about to run for its life will often empty its bowels first. Right there, before the sprint. It's not being rude. It's shedding a kilo or two so it's lighter and faster, and the mess it leaves behind disorients whatever's chasing. Clever, actually. Which is also, by the way, why your stomach does that lurching thing before you walk on stage, or into a big pitch, or hit send on the scary email. Your body is prepping to flee a predator. There is no predator. There is a Zoom link.

Your body doesn't know that. To your body, everything is dangerous. It genuinely does not distinguish between business risk and bodily risk. A hostile leopard and a hostile email land in the same ancient inbox.

The part almost nobody says out loud

Now hold that thought, because here's the part almost nobody says out loud, and it's the whole reason so many successful people are quietly falling apart while looking, on paper, like they've won.

Business rewards fight and flight.

Think about what actually gets applauded. The founder who's always closing. The one answering Slack at midnight. The one who fought the vendor down twenty percent and didn't blink. The one who ships and ships and ships, who's "so driven," who "just doesn't stop." We hand out revenue, respect, and LinkedIn followers for exactly the states an animal only enters when it thinks it's about to die.

Fight, in the wild, is a body flooded with adrenaline, ready to attack, tunnel-visioned, jaw set. In business we call that decisive and hungry and a killer. Flight is a body that cannot sit still, scanning for the exit, moving moving moving. In business we call that hustle and drive and a machine. We took the two states the body reserves for mortal danger and built an entire economy that pays for them.

And it works. That's the trap. It genuinely works, for a while. You get the client, the round, the growth chart that goes up and to the right. Your body concludes, quite reasonably, that this permanently-activated thing is a fantastic way to live, because look at the results. So it stays there. It moves in and never leaves. And the applause keeps confirming the strategy.

I know this one from the inside. I've got an engineering degree, I did procurement at Toyota, I co-founded a funded startup — I have spent a large chunk of my life in high-functioning fight-and-flight and getting paid for it. From the outside it looked like ambition. From the inside my shoulders lived somewhere around my ears and my jaw was doing quiet damage I wouldn't get the invoice for until years later.

There is always an invoice

Your body never sends the bill in real time. It runs a tab. You get the reward now — the closed deal, the shipped feature, the reputation as someone who gets things done — and the cost gets deferred, quietly, into a future you're too busy to look at. Success first. Bill later. And the bill, when it lands, doesn't say "nervous system, eleven years." It says back pain. It says you're at your kid's dinner physically but your head is three emails away. It says you can't sleep, or you can't feel much of anything, or you achieved the thing you spent a decade chasing and felt a strange flat nothing where the joy was supposed to be.

Mine arrived as a back that no physio, no yoga teacher, no amount of stretching could fix, because there was nothing to fix in the muscle. The muscle was just holding a bracing pattern I'd been rewarded for my whole career. My body had been in fight-flight for so long that "relaxed" had become a foreign language it no longer spoke. And then, when the tab finally came due, I spent a long stretch of years partly in shutdown — freeze, the state after the sprint, when the body has finally decided the danger won't stop and there's no point running anymore. That's the other end of the curve. You can only come down the way you went up, and if you never come down on purpose, the body eventually does it for you, and it is not gentle about it.

The problem is not that you have fight and flight in you. Thank god you do. The problem is living there.

This is the bit I need you to really hear, because it changes the whole frame: healthy fight is a boundary — a clean no, a price held, a standard defended. Healthy flight is shipping — real momentum toward something that matters. These states are not the enemy. They're gorgeous tools when you can visit them and come home. It's the not-coming-back that's the problem. It's the permanent residence in a state the body designed for emergencies, subsidised by a culture that keeps slipping you cash to stay.

So the work isn't to become calm and slow and stop caring about your business. Please. That's the fantasy of every burnt-out founder and it would bore you to death inside a week. The work is to become a nervous system that can move — fire up hard when the moment calls for it, then actually land afterwards. Regulated doesn't mean calm. It means you can go up and you can come down, on purpose, by choice, instead of getting stuck at the top until the body pulls the plug.

You don't have to earn your way out of this by achieving more. Achieving more is what got the tab so high. You have to learn, probably for the first time, what "down" even feels like in your own body, so it stops being a country you only visit by collapse.
