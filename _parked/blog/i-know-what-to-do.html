The pattern beneath

I know exactly what to do. So why am I not doing it?

The knowing-doing gap isn't a discipline problem. It's a nervous system event, and once you see it you can't unsee it.

You know the sentence. You've probably said it out loud, alone, in a slightly annoyed voice.

Six in the morning, cortisol already up before the alarm. Or a Sunday night, the proposal still open in a tab, and you catch yourself thinking: I know exactly what to do here. I've known for weeks. So why the hell am I not doing it?

I want to take that question seriously, because almost everyone answers it wrong. They answer it with insults. I'm lazy. I'm undisciplined. I self-sabotage. I need better habits, more accountability, a stricter morning routine, maybe a cold shower and a man in a headset yelling at me on YouTube.

None of that is the answer, and deep down you know it, because you've tried all of it and here you still are.

A different answer

So let me offer a different answer. The gap between knowing and doing is not a knowledge gap. If it were a knowledge gap, information would close it, and information has never once closed it for you. The gap is a nervous system gap. Your mind knows the move. Your body has cast a different vote. And the body votes first.

Picture the animal thing for a second, because it's the cleanest way in. An animal in the wild, calm, grazing, is in what we'd call a regulated state — settled, clear, able to see the whole field. Then something dangerous shows up and the body takes over. Not the mind. The body. Heart rate up, blood into the legs, ready to fight. If fighting won't work, run. If you can't outrun it, freeze — drop, go still, play dead. None of that is a decision. It's older than decisions. It happens in the time it takes your prefrontal cortex to clear its throat.

Now swap the leopard for the thing you're not doing. The price you keep not raising. The hire you keep not making. The email that's been a draft since the equinox. Your rational mind files these under decisions. Your body files them under predator. And when the body has flagged something as a threat, it will not let you calmly walk toward it just because a spreadsheet says you should. It'll do what it does with any threat: get you to fight it, flee it, or freeze in front of it. Which, for a founder, looks like arguing with the market, staying frantically busy with everything except the thing, or opening the document and going strangely, foggily blank.

I felt this in my own body for years before I understood it. Mine showed up as back pain. Chronic, stubborn, the kind no physio or yoga teacher could fix, because it wasn't structural — it was a body that had been braced against something for so long it forgot how to put the load down. I spent a small fortune on people trying to fix a spine that wasn't actually broken. The spine was fine. The nervous system underneath it was doing the equivalent of clenching a fist for years and calling it my personality.

Three costumes, one wardrobe

Here's where it gets interesting, and where a lot of people find themselves — because the classic version of this gap, "I know the one thing and I freeze on it," is only one costume. Same nervous system, different outfits.

Some of you aren't frozen at all. You're the opposite. You're doing everything — furiously, brilliantly, sixty hours a week — everything except the one move that actually matters. You're not stuck; you're a blur. But notice: the blur has a hole in the middle exactly the shape of the scary thing. That's not productivity. That's flight wearing a productivity costume, and it's arguably harder to catch, because it comes with applause.

And some of you can't even name the thing. If I asked you "what's the one move you're avoiding," you'd honestly say I don't know — the whole field has gone grey and undifferentiated, no single item stands out because your system has turned the contrast all the way down. That greyness isn't confusion about business. It's often freeze, dressed as being reasonable and open-minded. The body blurred the target so you wouldn't have to feel it clearly.

Frozen on the one thing. Sprinting around the one thing. Unable to see the one thing. Three costumes, one wardrobe. Same system, doing its ancient job: keeping you away from what it has decided is dangerous.

What actually works

So what do you actually do with that?

Not "push through." Pushing through is asking a braced body to sprint, and a braced body sprinting just injures itself and calls it Monday. You don't out-discipline a threat response. You out-safety it. You change the conditions until your body stops reading the move as a leopard, and then the move becomes almost boring to do. I've watched a founder who'd sat on a price increase for six months send the email in ninety seconds once her nervous system finally agreed it was safe. The email didn't get easier. Her body did.

That's the actual work. Find where the body has flagged the move as danger, understand what it once kept you safe from — because it did keep you safe, once, or it wouldn't still be running — and design the conditions where doing the thing feels less like walking toward a predator. It's less romantic than "unleash your inner beast" and considerably more effective.

The good news buried in all this: you were never broken and you were never lazy. Every hour you've spent calling yourself undisciplined was an hour spent mislabelling a nervous system doing exactly what evolution built it to do. You've been trying to win a body fight with a mind strategy, and the body was always going to win, because it's playing a game that's a few hundred million years older.

The knowing was never the problem. You've had the knowing the whole time.
