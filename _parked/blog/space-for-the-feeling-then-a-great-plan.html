Manifesto

Give space to the feeling. Then make a great plan.

Against "f*** your feelings, follow the plan." Also against coaching that's all feelings and no plan. The whole method, in one essay.

There are two camps in the self-improvement world and they both drive me a little mad.

Camp one has a man with veneers and a headset, and the message is: f*** your feelings, follow the plan. Discipline is destiny. Emotions are for the weak. You don't feel your way to a six-pack or a seven-figure business, you grind, you push, you dominate the day. Feelings are a soft thing, a thing that gets in the way of execution.

Camp two is gentler and smells faintly of palo santo. The message here is: just feel your feelings. Sit with it. Honour the emotion. Journal about the resistance. And... that's kind of it. The plan is treated as a slightly vulgar afterthought, because we don't want to be in our masculine capitalist trauma about achievement, do we.

Both camps are half-right, which is the most annoying way to be wrong.

Camp one is right that at some point you have to actually do the thing. A feeling that never becomes a move is just weather. You can be the most emotionally literate person on the island and still have a business that quietly dies of inaction. Feelings without a plan is a diary, not a company.

Camp two is right that you cannot bully a body into sustained performance. You can override it for a while — I did, for years — and the override works right up until the day it spectacularly doesn't, and then you're a high achiever with a back that won't unclench and a strange numbness where your motivation used to be. Plan without feeling isn't discipline. It's a debt you're taking out against your own nervous system, at a brutal interest rate.

The whole method in one line

So here's my actual position, and it's the whole method in one line: give space to the feeling, then make a great plan.

In that order. Both parts. Neither one optional.

I'm an engineer by training and a musician by nature, and I think that's why this makes such intuitive sense to me. A musician who can't feel anything plays technically perfect notes that move nobody. An engineer who won't build a plan just has feelings about bridges while the river stays uncrossed. You need the mammal and the machine. The mammal to know what's true, the machine to do something about it.

Let me show you the sequence, because the order isn't decorative — it's load-bearing.

You sit down to do the scary business move. Send the price. Make the hire. Fire the client. And your body does its thing — the chest tightens, the stomach drops, the shoulders start climbing toward your ears. Camp one says: ignore that, it's noise, push through. But you can't actually ignore it, so what you really do is either force yourself through it (bracing, which the body remembers and resists harder next time) or you quietly avoid the whole thing and go reorganise your Notion. Camp two says: oh, honour that feeling, sit with it, maybe it's not the right time. And you sit with it so respectfully that it's now nine months later and you've honoured yourself right out of a launch.

Space, then plan

Watch what "space, then plan" does instead.

First, space. You don't push the feeling down and you don't marry it. You give it about ninety seconds of honest attention. Feet on the floor. One long exhale, longer than the inhale — that alone tells your body the emergency is over. You notice the tight chest and instead of fighting it you get curious about it. Where exactly is it? What shape? Is it familiar — when else does this exact thing show up? You're not processing childhood. You're not going to cry into a cushion. You're just letting the body have its vote heard, because a vote that gets heard stops shouting. Ninety seconds. Two minutes, tops. This isn't a retreat, it's a pit stop.

The tightness settles, not because you defeated it but because you stopped treating it as an enemy. The body reads: okay, he's not ignoring me and he's not running, maybe this isn't a leopard. And your field of view widens back out. That narrow, tunnel-vision, everything-is-danger perspective softens into something you could actually think inside of.

Feel first, then plan hard. The feeling makes the plan wise. The plan makes the feeling worth having.

Then, and only then, the plan. And now the plan is sharp, because you're making it from a settled system instead of a sprinting one. From here you can see what actually matters. From here the move can be sized correctly — small enough that the body agrees to it, real enough that it counts. From here you make a genuinely good plan and, crucially, you can execute it, because you didn't build it on top of a body screaming that it's about to die.

This is the opposite of both camps and it borrows the best of each. It's got camp one's respect for execution — we are absolutely going to make the plan and do the thing, this is a business, the plan has to make money or the rest is an expensive feeling. And it's got camp two's respect for the body — we are not going to bully a mammal and call it discipline. It's engineer and mammal, in sequence, on purpose.

The reason most people can't do this on their own isn't that the idea is complicated. The idea is simple. It's that you can't easily give your own body space and stay clear-eyed enough to plan at the same time, especially when your system is activated — because the whole point of activation is that it hijacks the part of you that would do the planning. You need to have felt "settled" enough times that you can find your way back to it. It's a reference point you build, not a switch you flip.
